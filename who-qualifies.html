Who Qualifies for the Overtime Tax Deduction?

The No Tax on Overtime deduction can save eligible workers thousands per year — but only certain workers qualify. Here's exactly who's in, who's out, and why.

Calculate Your Savings →
Quick Answer

You qualify if you're a W-2 employee classified as non-exempt under the FLSA, you receive overtime pay at time-and-a-half for hours over 40/week, your MAGI is below the phase-out threshold ($150K single / $300K MFJ), and you don't file as Married Filing Separately. Salaried exempt workers, 1099 contractors, and gig workers do not qualify.

Who Qualifies: The Four Eligibility Tests

The "No Tax on Overtime" deduction was created by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21), signed July 4, 2025, and codified at Internal Revenue Code Section 225. It lets eligible workers deduct their FLSA overtime premium from federal taxable income for tax years 2025 through 2028. To claim it on your 2025 federal return, you must pass all four of these tests:

1. You must be a W-2 employee

The deduction is restricted to wage earners who receive a Form W-2. If you're paid via 1099-NEC, 1099-MISC, or any self-employment arrangement, you cannot claim this deduction — even if you work more than 40 hours per week. Gig workers (rideshare drivers, delivery couriers, freelance contractors) are excluded.

2. You must be FLSA non-exempt

The Fair Labor Standards Act divides employees into two categories: non-exempt (entitled to overtime pay) and exempt (not entitled to overtime). Only non-exempt workers qualify. This is the most misunderstood eligibility rule. If you're unsure of your status, check your offer letter, your pay stub (often labeled "NE" or "E"), or ask HR. Being paid hourly is a strong indicator of non-exempt status but not a guarantee.

3. Your income must be under the phase-out cap

The deduction begins phasing out at modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) of $150,000 for single and head-of-household filers, and $300,000 for married filing jointly. The reduction is $100 per $1,000 of MAGI above the threshold, fully eliminating the deduction at $275,000 (single/HOH) or $550,000 (MFJ).

4. You cannot file as Married Filing Separately

MFS filers are statutorily barred from claiming the deduction. To use the larger $25,000 MFJ cap, you and your spouse must file jointly.

Common misconception

Working overtime doesn't automatically qualify you. The deduction applies only to the FLSA overtime premium — the extra half-time portion of time-and-a-half pay. If your employer pays you the same hourly rate for hours 41+ as for hours 1–40, there's no FLSA premium and nothing to deduct.

What Counts and How Much You Can Deduct

Even if you pass all four eligibility tests, two more rules limit the size of your deduction.

Only the half-time premium counts

Federal overtime is paid at 1.5× your regular rate, but only the 0.5× portion is deductible. The base 1.0× — what you'd have earned at straight time — is not. The math is straightforward:

Deductible amount = Regular hourly rate × 0.5 × Overtime hours worked

For example, a nurse earning $40/hour who works 200 overtime hours in a year would have a deductible OT premium of $40 × 0.5 × 200 = $4,000.

Annual caps apply

The deduction is capped at $12,500 for single and head-of-household filers, and $25,000 for married filing jointly. Anything above the cap is non-deductible. Above the income phase-out thresholds, the cap is reduced dollar-for-dollar.

What this deduction does NOT reduce

  • FICA taxes (6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare) still apply to your full overtime pay.
  • State income taxes are unaffected — see our state conformity guide.
  • Local taxes (city, county) follow state rules.
How to verify it on your W-2

For tax year 2025, employers may report qualified overtime in Box 14 (voluntary, transitional). Starting tax year 2026, it must appear in Box 12 with code TT. See our step-by-step W-2 guide for screenshots and examples.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I qualify if I'm a salaried employee?
Only if you are classified as FLSA non-exempt and receive overtime pay at the federal time-and-a-half rate. Most salaried employees are FLSA exempt and do not qualify, regardless of how many hours they work. Check your offer letter or ask HR whether you're classified as exempt or non-exempt under the Fair Labor Standards Act. Some salaried roles (notably "salaried non-exempt" positions) do receive overtime — those workers can qualify.
Can independent contractors or 1099 workers claim the deduction?
No. The deduction under IRC Section 225 applies only to W-2 employees who are non-exempt under the FLSA. Independent contractors, gig workers, freelancers, and anyone receiving a 1099-NEC or 1099-MISC for their work cannot claim this deduction, even if they regularly work more than 40 hours per week. Self-employment income falls outside the FLSA framework entirely.
What if I'm married filing separately?
Taxpayers using Married Filing Separately (MFS) are statutorily barred from claiming the overtime deduction. If you and your spouse both work overtime and want to take advantage of this benefit, you must file jointly — the MFJ cap is $25,000 versus $12,500 for single and head of household. Run the numbers both ways before deciding, since other tax considerations may apply.
Does the deduction reduce my Social Security and Medicare taxes?
No. The OBBBA overtime deduction reduces only your federal income tax. FICA taxes (6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare = 7.65% total) still apply to your full overtime earnings. State and local income taxes are also unaffected unless your state separately conforms to the federal rule, and as of 2026 no state has done so. Use our main calculator to see your true after-tax savings.
What income level disqualifies me from the overtime deduction?
The deduction phases out at higher incomes. For single filers and head of household, it begins reducing at $150,000 MAGI and is fully eliminated at $275,000. For married filing jointly, the phase-out runs from $300,000 to $550,000. The reduction is $100 for every $1,000 of MAGI above the threshold. Most hourly workers fall well below these caps and receive the full benefit.
