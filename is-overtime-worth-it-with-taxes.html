Is Overtime Worth It With Taxes? Here's What You Actually Keep

The short answer is yes — overtime is almost always worth working, and the new OBBBA deduction makes it even more so. Here's exactly what you keep per overtime hour after federal tax, FICA, and the new deduction.

● Updated May 2026 ● 8 min read ● Covers OBBBA, FICA, and state taxes

One of the most common questions from hourly workers is: "Is overtime even worth it after taxes?" The short answer is yes, almost always — and in 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, P.L. 119-21, IRC §225) has made overtime financially even more attractive by allowing eligible W-2 workers to deduct the overtime premium from federal taxable income.

But let's go beyond the headline and look at the actual numbers. After federal income tax, FICA (Social Security + Medicare), and the new OBBBA deduction, how much do you actually keep for each overtime hour worked? Our free calculator handles the full math — this guide explains the reasoning behind the numbers.

The Short Answer — Yes, and More So Now Under OBBBA

Even before the OBBBA, overtime was worth it financially. At a 22% federal bracket, you kept 78 cents of every overtime dollar (before FICA and state taxes). After the OBBBA, the 0.5× overtime premium — the half above your regular rate — is now deductible, which saves you an additional 22% of that premium. For a worker earning $30/hr, that's an extra $3.30 per overtime hour in federal tax savings.

The myth that "overtime isn't worth it because you lose it all to taxes" is simply wrong. Marginal tax rates work on individual dollars, not your whole paycheck. Working an overtime hour at $30/hr when you're in the 22% bracket means you keep at least $23.40 of that $30 after federal income tax alone (before FICA). The OBBBA deduction adds back even more.

The "Higher Tax Bracket" Myth Explained

Many workers avoid overtime because they believe that earning more money will "push them into a higher tax bracket" and they'll end up with less take-home pay. This is a fundamental misunderstanding of how marginal tax rates work.

The U.S. federal income tax system is marginal — meaning each tax rate applies only to the dollars within that bracket, not to all of your income. If you earn $45,000 and the 22% bracket starts at $44,725 (for 2025 single filers), only the dollars above $44,725 are taxed at 22%. All the income below is taxed at 12% or lower.

Working overtime can push some of your overtime dollars into a higher bracket, but only those specific dollars are taxed at the higher rate. The rest of your income stays in its original brackets. Your take-home pay always increases when you earn more — no tax structure in the U.S. causes you to take home less by earning more.

What OBBBA Changes — and What It Doesn't

The OBBBA overtime deduction is powerful but targeted. Here's what it affects and what it doesn't:

What OBBBA changes:

  • The 0.5× overtime premium (above your regular rate) is now deductible from federal taxable income for tax years 2025–2028.
  • This deduction is claimed on Schedule 1-A above the line, reducing your AGI.
  • The benefit is worth your marginal federal rate times the premium, up to the cap ($12,500 single / $25,000 MFJ).

What OBBBA does NOT change:

  • FICA taxes (7.65%) still apply to all overtime pay in full. Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%) are not affected by the deduction.
  • State income taxes still apply. As of 2026, no state has conformed to the federal deduction. In a 5% state income tax state, you still pay 5% on your full overtime earnings.
  • The full 1.0× base-rate portion of overtime is still taxable. Only the 0.5× premium is deductible. If you earn $30/hr and work 1 overtime hour, the $30 base portion is still fully taxable; only the $15 premium is deductible.

When Overtime Might Not Be Worth It — Rare Cases

For the vast majority of workers, overtime is financially beneficial. However, a few edge cases deserve attention:

  • Near the OBBBA phase-out threshold ($150K single / $300K MFJ MAGI): If overtime income pushes you into the phase-out range, part of your deduction is reduced. The overtime is still worth working — you just lose some of the OBBBA benefit, not the base overtime pay itself.
  • ACA marketplace health insurance subsidies (Premium Tax Credit): If you buy insurance through the ACA marketplace and receive subsidies, higher income can reduce those subsidies. A significant overtime income increase could increase your net health insurance costs. This is a real consideration but rarely makes overtime "not worth it" — it just reduces the net gain slightly.
  • Means-tested benefits: Workers receiving benefits based on income (SNAP, Medicaid, housing assistance) may see benefit reductions as overtime income rises. This is a household-specific analysis beyond the scope of tax calculations.

Even in these edge cases, you're never financially worse off by earning overtime pay. The question is how much of the overtime income you retain net of all considerations.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is overtime taxed at a higher rate than regular pay?

No. Overtime is taxed at the same marginal federal rate as other income in the same bracket. The confusion comes from payroll withholding — employers often withhold more from overtime checks because their payroll system projects your full-year earnings based on the bigger check. But your annual tax return evens this out, and excess withholding comes back as a refund. The effective tax rate on overtime dollars is the same as on regular wage dollars in the same income range.

Does overtime income cause all my wages to be taxed at a higher rate?

No. The U.S. uses a progressive marginal tax system where each rate applies only to the income within that bracket. If overtime pushes some of your income into a higher bracket, only those specific dollars are taxed at the higher rate — the rest of your income stays at the lower rates. You always take home more money by earning more, even with higher brackets.

Can overtime push me into a higher tax bracket?

Yes, but only the dollars above the bracket threshold are taxed at the higher rate. For example, if you're near the 22%/24% bracket boundary, overtime dollars above that line are taxed at 24% — but the rest of your income remains at 22% or lower. The OBBBA deduction partially offsets this by reducing the taxable amount of your overtime premium.

Does the OBBBA make overtime completely tax-free?

No. The OBBBA deduction covers only the 0.5× premium portion of your overtime pay. The base 1.0× portion remains fully taxable as ordinary wages. Additionally, FICA taxes (6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare = 7.65%) still apply to all overtime earnings, and no state has conformed to the federal deduction as of 2026. The OBBBA reduces federal income tax on the premium portion — it does not eliminate all taxes on overtime.

How much more per overtime hour does the OBBBA deduction give me?

At the 22% bracket with $30/hr, your deductible premium per OT hour is $15 (30 × 0.5). The OBBBA saves you $15 × 22% = $3.30 per overtime hour in federal income tax. At $45/hr in the 22% bracket, the saving is $22.50 × 22% = $4.95 per hour. Use our calculator for your exact rate and situation.

Is overtime worth working in the 24% bracket?

Absolutely. In the 24% bracket, you keep 76% of every overtime dollar in federal income tax before the OBBBA (and before FICA). After the OBBBA deduction on the 0.5× premium, you keep even more of the overtime income. Combined with the base 1.5× pay itself, overtime consistently adds meaningfully to your take-home pay even at higher tax rates. For nurses, electricians, and other skilled trades workers often in the 22–24% bracket, overtime is one of the most impactful ways to increase annual income.
